The first question on almost every discovery call is some version of how much does this cost. I never answer it on that same call, and that is not a stalling tactic. It is the only way to give a price that survives contact with the actual project.

I should say upfront that I am biased here, I sell these projects, so take the philosophy below with that in mind. This is how I price AI automation work for small and mid size businesses, mostly CPA firms, tourism organizations, and operations still running on paper and spreadsheets: what actually moves the number, what a first month buys, and how to tell a serious quote from a guess dressed up as confidence.

## Why a Live Quote Is a Red Flag, Not a Convenience

A number given in the first few minutes of a call is a guess wearing a suit. Nobody can price a project accurately before seeing what data exists, what systems are already in use, and how much of the process lives in a spreadsheet versus in someone's memory. Answer how much before that homework is done, and the price ends up padded for the unknowns, or too low, which just means the real cost shows up later as change orders.

So the actual process is slower, and less satisfying in the moment. I study the requirements first: what systems the business runs, what data is usable as is, what is undocumented and needs to be reconstructed from someone's head. Only then does how much get an answer, and the answer has three parts, what is honestly deliverable, by when, and at what percentage of the full scope that first delivery represents. A client who hears this covers about sixty percent of what we discussed, in five weeks, for this range, is getting a real answer. A client who hears one flat number on the first call is getting a placeholder.

## What Actually Moves the Price

Four things move the cost of an automation project more than anything else, and the AI model being used is not one of them.

**How documented the process is.** A process that lives in one person's head costs more to automate than one that is written down, because the first billable step becomes interviewing that person. This is the same question that opens every discovery call I run: is it documented, or is it in someone's head. The answer changes the estimate before a single line of a proposal gets written.

**How many systems are involved.** Pulling data from one system is a different project than reconciling data across four systems that were never built to talk to each other. Every additional system is another point of failure and another block of work.

**How much of the data is usable as is.** Real client data is messier than a demo dataset, always. Migrating actual records, actual order numbers, actual client history costs more than building against sample data, but it is also the only way the system tells the truth on day one instead of on day ninety.

**What done means to the client.** A single automated workflow is a different project than a full operational system. Scope creep is usually not dishonesty, it is usually an undefined finish line from the start, and I have been on both sides of that mistake myself.

## Rough Ranges, Rounded and Honest

These are rounded, illustrative ranges for the kind of independent, senior led automation work described here, not a quote for any specific project. I am rounding on purpose. Every real number still needs the requirements conversation above first.

A single automated process, one workflow like drafting a specific type of client email or pulling one report automatically, tends to run in the low thousands and takes two to four weeks.

A full month one of a larger engagement, the twenty percent of the system that removes eighty percent of the pain, tends to run in the mid single digit thousands to around ten thousand dollars, depending on how many systems are involved.

A complete three month engagement, discovery through a working, documented system, tends to land somewhere between the low five figures and the high twenties, depending on scope and the number of systems touched.

An ongoing retainer after launch, for maintenance, small additions, and monthly reporting, tends to run a few thousand dollars a month.

Hourly senior expertise, brought in for a specific gap rather than a full build, tends to run somewhere in the low hundreds per hour, scoped in blocks rather than open ended.

Anyone quoting far below these ranges for comparable scope is usually skipping the discovery step, the documentation step, or both, and the client pays for it later, just later than the invoice. Anyone quoting far above them, for a small business with one or two systems and a defined process, is usually pricing for their own overhead rather than the work.

## What a Month One Actually Buys

The most common mistake I see in automation pricing is treating a three month engagement as three equal installments. It is not, and pricing it that way undersells month one, which is where almost all the value gets proven.

The method is simple to state and hard to execute. I identify the twenty percent of features that resolve eighty percent of the client's actual pain, and ship that first, against real data, inside the first month. Everything after that is expansion, not the proof that the system works.

I anchor scope for that first month to one flagship requirement, usually the client, department, or process whose needs are demanding enough that solving for them also covers everyone else on the list, whether that is a furniture factory's most complex production line or a CPA firm's busiest quarter. Build for the hardest real case first, and the easier cases are mostly covered by the same system.

What a client should expect to see by the end of month one is a working piece of the system running on their actual data, not a demo. That is not the full scope, and I say that upfront too. It is proof that the approach works, priced as the highest value delivery in the engagement rather than as one third of the total.

## Build vs. Buy vs. Rent

Not every automation need justifies a custom build, and I say that even though building is how I make a living.

**Build** makes sense when the process is specific enough that no off the shelf tool fits it well, and the volume of repetitive work justifies the upfront cost against the time it saves every month going forward.

**Buy** makes sense when a SaaS tool already solves eighty percent of the need at a fraction of the cost of a custom build. Recommending an existing tool instead of building one is not leaving money on the table, it is the honest answer for that specific gap.

**Rent** makes sense when a project needs expertise that is not needed permanently, a specific integration, a security review, a narrow technical domain outside the core work. Bringing in senior expertise by the hour for that gap costs less than hiring for it, and less than faking it. The client pays for judgment on that one problem, not for a new headcount.

A pricing conversation that only offers build is not fully honest about the other two options, and it usually costs the client more than the problem was worth.

## How to Spot a Bad Quote

A few honest signals, from the other side of the table.

If a price shows up before any real conversation about requirements, there was no discovery and no documentation review, so there is nothing yet to base a number on. A single flat number with no breakdown of what month one delivers versus what comes later usually means nobody separated the proof of concept from the rest of the build. And if nobody asks whether the process is written down or lives in someone's head, the estimate is guessing at the hardest part of the work.

The last one I would watch for is a twelve month contract signed before a single month of work has been delivered. A serious engagement proves itself in the first phase before it asks for the rest.

## Getting Started

So the realistic first step is not requesting a quote. It is a conversation about the actual systems, the documentation gaps, and where the finish line is, before any number gets attached to it. A price that comes out of that kind of conversation is a plan. A price from a short, rushed call is just a guess with a dollar sign on it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does a small AI automation project typically cost?

A single automated workflow tends to run in the low thousands and take two to four weeks. A full three month engagement covering a broader system tends to land in the low five figures to the high twenties, depending on how many systems are involved and how documented the process was to start.

### Why won't a serious automation consultant give a price on the first call?

Because an accurate price depends on how documented the process is, how many systems are involved, and how usable the existing data is, none of which is known in the first few minutes of a call. A number given before that homework is done is either padded or too low.

### What does the first month of an automation engagement actually deliver?

The twenty percent of the system that resolves eighty percent of the client's pain, built and shipped against the client's real data, not a demo. It is the highest value month of the engagement, not an equal third of the total scope.

### Should a small business build a custom automation or buy an existing tool?

It depends on fit. If an existing SaaS tool already covers most of the need, buying it is usually cheaper and faster than building. Custom builds make sense when no off the shelf option fits the process well and the volume of repetitive work justifies the cost.

### What does it mean to "rent" AI expertise instead of building or buying?

It means bringing in senior expertise by the hour for a specific gap, an integration, a security review, rather than hiring a full time role or attempting it without the right background. The client pays for judgment on one problem, not for permanent headcount.

### What is a red flag in an AI automation quote?

A flat price with no requirements conversation behind it, no clear breakdown of what month one delivers, and no question about whether the process being automated is actually documented anywhere. Those three gaps usually mean the estimate is a guess.
